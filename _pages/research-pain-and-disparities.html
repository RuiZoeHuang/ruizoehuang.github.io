Welfare policies, joint pain prevalence and educational gaps in 50 U.S. states from 2011 to 2021: A fixed effects analysis ↗

The Journal of Pain

Data and Method:

  • Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System, 2011–2021: six biennial waves covering all 50 U.S. states, linked to state welfare-policy measures.
  • Logistic regression to estimate pain trends and educational gaps; state and year fixed effects to examine policy associations.

Highlights:

  • Joint pain increased in most states, and educational gaps widened in more than half.
  • More generous Medicaid programs were associated with lower pain prevalence overall and among adults with the least education.
  • The association with smaller educational gaps was marginal, highlighting both the promise and limits of policy-related differences.

Educational disparities in joint pain within and across US states: Do macro sociopolitical contexts matter? ↗

PAIN

Data and Method:

  • 2017 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System: 407,938 adults ages 25–80, linked to state policy, income-inequality, and social-cohesion measures.
  • Multilevel logistic regression examining state variation in joint pain and its educational gradient.

Highlights:

  • Both pain prevalence and educational disparities varied substantially across states.
  • Greater SNAP generosity and social cohesion were associated with lower overall pain prevalence; income inequality was associated with wider educational disparities.
  • Residents across education levels faced greater pain risk in states with larger educational gaps.

Country-level gender inequality and sex disparities in adolescent chronic pain: A 47-country analysis ↗

Preventive Medicine

Data and Method:

  • 2017/18 Health Behaviour in School-aged Children study: 244,097 adolescents ages 11–15 across 47 countries, linked to national gender-inequality and intimate-partner-violence indicators.
  • Modified Poisson regression for relative disparities and multilevel linear probability models for absolute disparities.

Highlights:

  • About 44% of adolescents reported chronic pain, with higher prevalence among girls.
  • Greater national gender inequality was associated with more pain but narrower sex gaps because boys’ pain increased more steeply.
  • Higher prevalence of violence against women was associated with wider absolute female–male pain gaps.

Chronic pain prevalence and trends in urban, suburban, and rural areas among American adults aged 55+, 1998–2022 ↗

Social Science & Medicine

Data and Method:

  • Health and Retirement Study, 1998–2022: 35,230 adults ages 55 and older and 206,600 person-wave observations.
  • Modified Poisson models and subgroup analyses comparing place-based pain prevalence and change over time.

Highlights:

  • Pain prevalence rose by approximately 70% over 24 years and remained highest in rural areas.
  • Suburbs experienced the fastest increase, approaching rural pain levels by 2022.
  • Particularly rapid increases among suburban residents in the South identify an important focus for pain surveillance.

Managing existing pain: Predicting pain progression and improvement using machine learning approaches ↗

Annals of Epidemiology

Data and Method:

  • 2019–2020 National Health Interview Survey Longitudinal Cohort, following four one-year transitions in pain severity and improvement.
  • Random forests with permutation-based feature importance; analyses stratified by demographic, socioeconomic, and urban–rural groups.

Highlights:

  • The number of coexisting conditions predicted every pain transition across nearly all subgroups.
  • Important predictors differed by transition and population: employment and social relationships were especially salient in particular groups.
  • Treatment use and social and economic circumstances became more important for progression to high-impact pain; these predictive associations do not establish causation.

Social network change after new-onset pain among middle-aged and older European adults ↗

Social Science & Medicine

Data and Method:

  • Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe, 2011–2015: 12,647 adults ages 51 and older in 11 countries.
  • Four-year change-score analyses comparing adults with new-onset chronic pain with those who remained pain-free.

Highlights:

  • New mild or moderate pain was associated with larger and more diverse social networks, consistent with support mobilization.
  • Network growth did not necessarily mean better relationships: satisfaction or closeness declined for some pain groups.
  • Social consequences varied with pain severity and sex, with greater declines in some relationship measures among men.

Over 50 years of research on social disparities in pain and pain treatment: A scoping review of reviews ↗

PAIN

Data and Method:

  • 397 peer-reviewed reviews spanning 56 years, identified through six bibliographic databases after screening 4,432 unique records.
  • Scoping review mapping social groups, pain outcomes, proposed mechanisms, and policy or practice recommendations.

Highlights:

  • Research concentrated on sex/gender, race/ethnicity, and age; socioeconomic and geographic disparities received less attention.
  • Explanations and recommendations focused much more on individuals than on structural conditions.
  • The review calls for greater attention to prevention, lower-income countries, and macrolevel drivers of pain inequality.

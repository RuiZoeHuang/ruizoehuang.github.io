<section id="contextualizing-health" class="research-topic" aria-labelledby="contextualizing-health-heading">
<h2 id="contextualizing-health-heading">Contextualizing health and health inequalities</h2>
<article class="research-block">
<h3><a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpain.2025.105539" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Welfare policies, joint pain prevalence and educational gaps in 50 U.S. states from 2011 to 2021: A fixed effects analysis<span aria-hidden="true"> ↗</span></a></h3>
<p class="research-publication">The Journal of Pain · 2025 · 36:105539</p>
<h4>Data and Method:</h4>
<ul><li>Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System, 2011–2021: six biennial waves covering all 50 U.S. states, linked to state welfare-policy measures.</li><li>Logistic regression to estimate pain trends and educational gaps; state and year fixed effects to examine policy associations.</li></ul>
<h4>Highlights:</h4>
<ul><li>Joint pain increased in most states, and educational gaps widened in more than half.</li><li>More generous Medicaid programs were associated with lower pain prevalence overall and among adults with the least education.</li><li>The association with smaller educational gaps was marginal, highlighting both the promise and limits of policy-related differences.</li></ul>
</article>
<article class="research-block">
<h3><a href="https://doi.org/10.1097/j.pain.0000000000002945" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Educational disparities in joint pain within and across US states: Do macro sociopolitical contexts matter?<span aria-hidden="true"> ↗</span></a></h3>
<p class="research-publication">PAIN · 2023 · 164(10):2358–2369</p>
<h4>Data and Method:</h4>
<ul><li>2017 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System: 407,938 adults ages 25–80, linked to state policy, income-inequality, and social-cohesion measures.</li><li>Multilevel logistic regression examining state variation in joint pain and its educational gradient.</li></ul>
<h4>Highlights:</h4>
<ul><li>Both pain prevalence and educational disparities varied substantially across states.</li><li>Greater SNAP generosity and social cohesion were associated with lower overall pain prevalence; income inequality was associated with wider educational disparities.</li><li>Residents across education levels faced greater pain risk in states with larger educational gaps.</li></ul>
</article>
<article class="research-block">
<h3><a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2026.108495" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Country-level gender inequality and sex disparities in adolescent chronic pain: A 47-country analysis<span aria-hidden="true"> ↗</span></a></h3>
<p class="research-publication">Preventive Medicine · 2026 · 204:108495</p>
<h4>Data and Method:</h4>
<ul><li>2017/18 Health Behaviour in School-aged Children study: 244,097 adolescents ages 11–15 across 47 countries, linked to national gender-inequality and intimate-partner-violence indicators.</li><li>Modified Poisson regression for relative disparities and multilevel linear probability models for absolute disparities.</li></ul>
<h4>Highlights:</h4>
<ul><li>About 44% of adolescents reported chronic pain, with higher prevalence among girls.</li><li>Greater national gender inequality was associated with more pain but narrower sex gaps because boys’ pain increased more steeply.</li><li>Higher prevalence of violence against women was associated with wider absolute female–male pain gaps.</li></ul>
</article>
<article class="research-block">
<h3><a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2025.118625" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Chronic pain prevalence and trends in urban, suburban, and rural areas among American adults aged 55+, 1998–2022<span aria-hidden="true"> ↗</span></a></h3>
<p class="research-publication">Social Science &amp; Medicine · 2025 · 385:118625</p>
<h4>Data and Method:</h4>
<ul><li>Health and Retirement Study, 1998–2022: 35,230 adults ages 55 and older and 206,600 person-wave observations.</li><li>Modified Poisson models and subgroup analyses comparing place-based pain prevalence and change over time.</li></ul>
<h4>Highlights:</h4>
<ul><li>Pain prevalence rose by approximately 70% over 24 years and remained highest in rural areas.</li><li>Suburbs experienced the fastest increase, approaching rural pain levels by 2022.</li><li>Particularly rapid increases among suburban residents in the South identify an important focus for pain surveillance.</li></ul>
</article>
<article class="research-block">
<h3><a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssmph.2022.101069" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Health and health behaviors in China: Anomalies in the SES-health gradient?<span aria-hidden="true"> ↗</span></a></h3>
<p class="research-publication">SSM – Population Health · 2022 · 17:101069</p>
<h4>Data and Method:</h4>
<ul><li>2015 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study: 14,420 adults ages 45 and older.</li><li>Regression models of self-rated health and chronic conditions, with Karlson–Holm–Breen decomposition of smoking, drinking, and overweight.</li></ul>
<h4>Highlights:</h4>
<ul><li>Higher education and wealth were linked to better self-rated health and less arthritis, but advantages were not consistent across conditions.</li><li>Some conditions and behavioral risks were as common—or more common—among higher-status adults.</li><li>The findings show why cultural, historical, and political context matters when applying Fundamental Cause Theory beyond Western settings.</li></ul>
</article>
</section>
<section id="risk-resilience" class="research-topic" aria-labelledby="risk-resilience-heading">
<h2 id="risk-resilience-heading">Risk and Resilience over the Life Course</h2>
<article class="research-block">
<h3><a href="https://doi.org/10.1177/00221465251401249" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Early-Life War Exposure and Later-Life Chronic Pain in Vietnam: Risk, Resilience, and Timing<span aria-hidden="true"> ↗</span></a></h3>
<p class="research-publication">Journal of Health and Social Behavior · 2026 · OnlineFirst</p>
<h4>Data and Method:</h4>
<ul><li>2018 Vietnam Health and Aging Study: 1,826 older adults with retrospective information about wartime experiences.</li><li>Regression and Karlson–Holm–Breen mediation analyses; comparisons across cohorts exposed in childhood, adolescence, or young adulthood.</li></ul>
<h4>Highlights:</h4>
<ul><li>Early-life war exposure was associated with greater risk of moderate or severe pain decades later.</li><li>War-related physical and mental health problems accounted for much of the association, alongside protective processes such as social engagement.</li><li>Those exposed as children were more vulnerable to wartime violence than the older exposure cohorts.</li></ul>
</article>
</section>
<section id="other-topics" class="research-topic" aria-labelledby="other-topics-heading">
<h2 id="other-topics-heading">Other Topics</h2>
<article class="research-block">
<h3><a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annepidem.2026.110297" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Managing existing pain: Predicting pain progression and improvement using machine learning approaches<span aria-hidden="true"> ↗</span></a></h3>
<p class="research-publication">Annals of Epidemiology · 2026 · Online publication · Article 110297</p>
<h4>Data and Method:</h4>
<ul><li>2019–2020 National Health Interview Survey Longitudinal Cohort, following four one-year transitions in pain severity and improvement.</li><li>Random forests with permutation-based feature importance; analyses stratified by demographic, socioeconomic, and urban–rural groups.</li></ul>
<h4>Highlights:</h4>
<ul><li>The number of coexisting conditions predicted every pain transition across nearly all subgroups.</li><li>Important predictors differed by transition and population: employment and social relationships were especially salient in particular groups.</li><li>Treatment use and social and economic circumstances became more important for progression to high-impact pain; these predictive associations do not establish causation.</li></ul>
</article>
<article class="research-block">
<h3><a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2022.115215" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Social network change after new-onset pain among middle-aged and older European adults<span aria-hidden="true"> ↗</span></a></h3>
<p class="research-publication">Social Science &amp; Medicine · 2022 · 310:115215</p>
<h4>Data and Method:</h4>
<ul><li>Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe, 2011–2015: 12,647 adults ages 51 and older in 11 countries.</li><li>Four-year change-score analyses comparing adults with new-onset chronic pain with those who remained pain-free.</li></ul>
<h4>Highlights:</h4>
<ul><li>New mild or moderate pain was associated with larger and more diverse social networks, consistent with support mobilization.</li><li>Network growth did not necessarily mean better relationships: satisfaction or closeness declined for some pain groups.</li><li>Social consequences varied with pain severity and sex, with greater declines in some relationship measures among men.</li></ul>
</article>
<article class="research-block">
<h3><a href="https://doi.org/10.1177/08982643261492103" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Racial-Ethnic Differences in Healthcare Experience Among Middle-Aged and Older Adults in the United States: Evidence From Four Waves of the Health and Retirement Study<span aria-hidden="true"> ↗</span></a></h3>
<p class="research-publication">Journal of Aging and Health · 2026 · OnlineFirst</p>
<h4>Data and Method:</h4>
<ul><li>Health and Retirement Study, 2014–2020: 16,088 respondents across four waves, with measures of satisfaction, affordability, access, and whether care reflected patients’ preferences.</li><li>Survey-weighted multilevel logistic regression and interactions with Medicaid and Medicare coverage.</li></ul>
<h4>Highlights:</h4>
<ul><li>Racial and ethnic disparities differed across dimensions of healthcare experience, rather than following one uniform pattern.</li><li>Unmet care preferences remained more common among Black, Hispanic, and other minoritized respondents than among non-Hispanic White respondents.</li><li>Medicaid reduced some gaps, especially for Hispanic respondents; Medicare’s moderating role was more limited.</li></ul>
</article>
<article class="research-block">
<h3><a href="https://doi.org/10.1097/j.pain.0000000000003676" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Over 50 years of research on social disparities in pain and pain treatment: A scoping review of reviews<span aria-hidden="true"> ↗</span></a></h3>
<p class="research-publication">PAIN · 2025 · 166(11):2458–2472</p>
<h4>Data and Method:</h4>
<ul><li>397 peer-reviewed reviews spanning 56 years, identified through six bibliographic databases after screening 4,432 unique records.</li><li>Scoping review mapping social groups, pain outcomes, proposed mechanisms, and policy or practice recommendations.</li></ul>
<h4>Highlights:</h4>
<ul><li>Research concentrated on sex/gender, race/ethnicity, and age; socioeconomic and geographic disparities received less attention.</li><li>Explanations and recommendations focused much more on individuals than on structural conditions.</li><li>The review calls for greater attention to prevention, lower-income countries, and macrolevel drivers of pain inequality.</li></ul>
</article>
</section>
